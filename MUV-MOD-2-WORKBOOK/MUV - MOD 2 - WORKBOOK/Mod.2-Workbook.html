Lesson 2.1

Movement Story

Draft your four-part story arc, the foundation everything in this module is built on.

Why this matters: Without a clear story your organisation is just a service. A movement needs a narrative: a problem worth solving, a belief worth holding, a future worth building towards and a specific invitation to join in.

How to use this page: Complete all four boxes in order. Each part builds on the last. Write in plain language, not how it reads in a brochure, but how it sounds when you say it out loud to someone who has never heard of your organisation. Do this with one other person who knows your organisation well enough to push back on vague language. Takes 30 to 40 minutes. Bring your Fit Check from Lesson 1.5: your Belief Statement goes in Part 2.

The mechanics work identically whether you are a charity or a business. Wherever you see supporter/prospect, use whichever term fits your organisation: donor, customer, client, volunteer or member.

Your four-part story arc

Write your first honest draft. Good enough to say out loud is good enough to start.

Part 1The Problem

What is wrong with the world as it is? Write the injustice, gap or failure your organisation exists to address. Be specific. Avoid starting with "we".

Part 2The Belief

What do you believe to be true that others may not yet see? This is the conviction that drives your work. Use your Belief Statement from Lesson 1.5 as a starting point.

Part 3The Vision

What does the world look like when your belief becomes reality? Not a mission statement. A picture of change that people can imagine themselves inside.

Part 4The Invitation

What is the one specific thing you are asking someone to do right now? Not everything you offer, the single most important first step. Make it easy to say yes to.

Lesson 2.2

Supporter Roles Mapping

Name the roles in your movement, not just the demographics.

Why this matters: If you only think in demographics (age, income, job title) you miss the jobs people are actually trying to do in your movement. You either talk over them or under-ask them and they drift away. Role-based thinking tells you what someone needs at this moment in their journey, which is far more useful than knowing their postcode.

How to use this page: Complete this during or after Lesson 2.2. Have your Movement Story (Lesson 2.1) to hand. Aim for 3 roles minimum and 6 maximum. Use the universal role names below as a starting point, then give each role your own name, the word or phrase your people would actually recognise themselves in.

The mechanics work identically whether you are a charity or a business. Wherever you see supporter/prospect, use whichever term fits your organisation: donor, customer, client, volunteer or member.

The shift

Quick reference as you complete the table below.

✗Demographic thinking

Tells you who someone is: age, income, job title, location. Useful context, but it does not tell you what they need right now.

e.g. ‘Female, 35 to 55, urban professional’

✓Role-based thinking

Tells you where someone is in your movement right now. Tells you what they need to move forward. This is what changes what you say to them.

e.g. ‘First-Timer, needs a clear next step and welcome’

The roles are the same. The language of the invitation differs.

Lesson 2.3

Supporter Journey Map

Map the first contact to Advocate path and the next obvious step at every stage.

Why this matters: If people do not know what to do next, they will not stick or share. Without a journey map, your experiences are random and momentum leaks. You keep doing cold starts instead of building layers.

How to use this page: Bring your Movement Story (Lesson 2.1) and Supporter Roles (Lesson 2.2) before you start. Fill in all four columns for each stage. The ‘Next Obvious Step’ column is the most important: be specific. One action, not a list. Review this map monthly, not just once.

The mechanics work identically whether you are a charity or a business. Wherever you see supporter/prospect, use whichever term fits your organisation: donor, customer, client, volunteer or member.

Lesson 2.4

Invitation Rewrite

Turn your invitations into language real people will say, share and act on.

Why this matters: Even with a good story and a clear journey, if the language is full of organisational jargon or asks for too many things at once, supporters and prospects will not repeat it. The movement stalls at the organisation’s mouth.

How to use this page: Complete this during or after Lesson 2.4. Bring your Journey Map (Lesson 2.3) and look at the ‘Next Obvious Step’ column: those are the invitations you are rewriting here. Complete one block per role or journey stage. Aim for four rewrites minimum.

The mechanics work identically whether you are a charity or a business. Wherever you see supporter/prospect, use whichever term fits your organisation: donor, customer, client, volunteer or member.

Four principles

Hold every invitation against all four before it goes anywhere.

Short

One sentence, not a paragraph.

Concrete

Names a specific action or moment.

True

Plain human language, not mission-speak.

Theirs

Sounds like something they would say.

Three pass or fail tests

Apply to all four rewrite blocks before each one leaves this page.

Talk Test

Would a real supporter say this out loud to a friend, in a pub, on a school run, at a neighbour’s doorstep? Any hesitation at all? Simplify it.

One-Thing Test

Is there exactly one action being asked for? Not a menu. If you can spot ‘and’ in the invitation, cut one of the asks.

One-Role Test

Is the invitation written for this specific role at this specific stage, or could it belong to any role in Lesson 2.2? If generic, make it specific.
